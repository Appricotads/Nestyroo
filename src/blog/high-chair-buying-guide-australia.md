A high chair is one of those baby items you'll use several times a day for a couple of years, so it's worth choosing well. The right one is safe, easy to clean and fits your kitchen. The wrong one wobbles, hides food in every crevice and gets pushed into a corner.

This guide covers the types of high chair, the safety checks to do in Australia, and the features that are worth paying for. *Last updated 23 November 2026.*

**In this guide:** [When you need one](#when) · [Types](#types) · [Safety checks](#safety) · [Features that matter](#features) · [What to skip](#skip) · [When to buy](#timing) · [Checklist](#checklist) · [FAQ](#faq)

<h2 id="when">When do you need a high chair?</h2>

Most babies start solids at around six months, once they can sit with little or no support and show interest in food. That's usually when a high chair becomes useful. If you're expecting, you don't need to buy one before the birth, so it can wait until later in your baby's first six months.

<h2 id="types">Types of high chair</h2>

| Type | Best for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| **Full-size high chair** | A dedicated spot in the kitchen or dining area | Takes up space and may not fold |
| **Grow-with-me (convertible)** | Using it from baby to toddler and even child, as it changes into a chair | Check it's comfortable and safe at each stage, not just the first |
| **Compact or folding** | Small kitchens and flats | Check it's stable once unfolded, and that the folding lock is firm |
| **Booster seat** | Older babies and toddlers, strapped onto an adult chair | Needs a sturdy chair, and it isn't suitable for a baby who can't sit well |
| **Hook-on (clip-on) chair** | Travel, cafes and small spaces | Only for a table that suits it, and it must be slip-resistant and level |

If you're not sure, a simple full-size or convertible chair is the easiest to live with. A hook-on or booster is a good second seat, but it isn't a replacement for a stable high chair for most families.

<h2 id="safety">Safety checks before you buy</h2>

Price comes second to safety. Check these things:

- **The Australian Standard.** Check the chair meets AS 4684, which covers high chair safety requirements. Raising Children Network recommends this for new chairs. Look for it on the label or in the product details.
- **A five-point harness.** This means straps over the shoulders and hips and between the legs. Use it every time, even for short meals.
- **A stable base.** The chair should be sturdy and shouldn't tip when baby leans or pushes. A wider base helps.
- **Locking wheels and folding.** If the chair has wheels, they should lock. Any folding mechanism should lock firmly in position.
- **Placement.** Keep the chair away from walls, benches and tables that baby could push off.
- **Never leave baby alone** in a high chair, even for a moment.
- **Check for recalls.** Search the model on [Product Safety Australia](https://www.productsafety.gov.au/) before you buy, especially for second-hand chairs.

For hook-on chairs, check that the seat is slip-resistant and level before baby sits in it.

<h2 id="features">Features that are worth it</h2>

- **A wipeable surface.** Seats and trays with few seams and crevices are far easier to clean.
- **A removable tray**, ideally one that can go in the dishwasher or be rinsed quickly.
- **An adjustable height and footrest.** A footrest helps babies sit properly and makes meals easier.
- **A recline setting**, if you want to use the chair for younger babies.
- **A size that fits your space.** Measure the spot where it will go, and check the folded size if you'll store it.
- **A simple design.** Fewer parts means fewer places for food to hide and less to go wrong.

<h2 id="skip">What to skip</h2>

- **Complicated chairs with lots of attachments** you won't use.
- **Chairs you can't see in person or return easily.** Check the returns policy, especially for online purchases.
- **A very low price from an unfamiliar seller** with no safety information. Check the standard and the seller before you buy.
- **Buying more than you need.** One good chair is enough. A booster or hook-on can come later if you travel.

A high chair is a planned, mid-sized purchase, so it's worth comparing prices before you buy. Record the price at two or three retailers, then watch for sales. <h2 id="checklist">High chair checklist</h2>

- Meets AS 4684
- Five-point harness that you'll always use
- Stable base, with locking wheels and a firm folding lock if it has them
- Wipeable seat and an easy-to-clean tray
- Fits your space, folded or unfolded
- Recall check done
- Returns policy and delivery cost checked

<h2 id="faq">Frequently asked questions</h2>

### Do I need a high chair?
Most families find one useful once baby starts solids, since it keeps baby safely seated and the meal off the floor. It isn't essential for newborns.

### When should I buy a high chair?
Usually when baby is around five to six months and getting ready for solids. You don't need it before the birth.

### What Australian Standard applies to high chairs?
Check for AS 4684, which covers high chair safety requirements. Also check the product for recalls on Product Safety Australia.

### Can I use a booster seat instead of a high chair?
For an older baby who sits well, a booster on a sturdy adult chair can work. It isn't suitable for a baby who can't sit steadily, and a proper high chair is usually easier for the early months.

### Is a second-hand high chair safe?
It can be, if the harness works, the chair is stable, parts aren't missing or broken, and there are no recalls. If you can't check these, buy new.

### Are hook-on chairs safe?
They can be, if the seat is slip-resistant and level and the table is suitable. They're best as a travel or backup option.
