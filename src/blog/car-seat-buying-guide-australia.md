*Last updated 5 November 2026.*

Choosing a car seat is one of the biggest decisions you'll make before bub arrives. This car seat buying guide covers what the Australian Road Rules require, the types of seats, what to check before you buy and how to make sure it's fitted properly, so you can pick one that's safe, legal and right for your car.

<h2 id="law">What the rules say</h2>

Under the Australian Road Rules, every child must travel in an approved restraint that suits their age and size, and it must be fitted correctly. The rules are:

| Child's age | Type of restraint |
|---|---|
| Birth to 6 months | Rearward-facing restraint (capsule or convertible seat) |
| 6 months to 4 years | Rearward-facing, or forward-facing with an in-built harness |
| 4 to 7 years | Forward-facing with an in-built harness, or a booster seat |
| 7 years and over | Booster seat recommended until the adult seatbelt fits properly |

**Where kids can sit:**
- Children under 4 must not travel in the front row of a vehicle that has two or more rows of seats.
- Children aged 4 to under 7 can only sit in the front row if all the other seats are taken by children younger than 7.

Safety experts recommend keeping your child in each type of seat for as long as they fit it within the seat's limits. [Kidsafe](https://www.kidsafe.com.au) has more guidance.

<h2 id="types">Types of car seats</h2>

### Baby capsule
- Rearward-facing, for newborns and young babies.
- Usually clicks into a base fixed in your car, and often onto a pram frame.
- Outgrown fairly quickly, often within the first year, depending on how big your bub is.

### Convertible seat
- Starts rearward-facing, then converts to forward-facing as your child grows.
- Many models can be used from birth, so you may not need a capsule at all.
- Heavier and not portable, so it stays in the car.

### Forward-facing seat with harness
- For toddlers and older kids who have outgrown a rearward-facing seat.
- Some models convert to a booster later.

### Booster seat
- For older kids who have outgrown a harness seat but are still too small for the adult seatbelt.
- Helps the seatbelt sit across the child's body correctly.

<h2 id="what-to-check">What to check before you buy</h2>

1. **The standard.** Child car seats sold here must meet AS/NZS 1754. Look for the compliance label and markings on the seat and the box, and don't buy one that only meets an overseas standard.
2. **Your child's age, height and weight.** Check the seat's limits and the shoulder height markers.
3. **Fit in your car.** Not every seat suits every car. Check the anchor points, the shape of your back seat and how much room is left for other passengers.
4. **How it installs.** Does it use the seatbelt, or ISOFIX and top tether anchors? Check what your car supports.
5. **Ease of use.** Look at how the harness adjusts and how easy it is to buckle your child in. A seat you can use correctly every time is a safer seat.
6. **Independent ratings.** Look for independent crash and ease-of-use test results.
7. **Expiry date.** Seats have a use-by date set by the manufacturer. Check it.
8. **Warranty and support.** Find out who handles repairs and parts locally.

<h2 id="fitting">Getting the fit right</h2>

A great seat that's badly fitted isn't safe, and a lot of seats aren't installed properly.

- Follow the seat's instructions and your car's manual.
- Check that the harness is snug and sits at the right height.
- Make sure the seat only moves a small amount at the base.
- Have it checked or fitted by a qualified child restraint fitter. Kidsafe, some baby retailers and motoring clubs offer this.
- Recheck the fit as your child grows, and whenever you move the seat to a different car.

<h2 id="second-hand">Second-hand seats: be careful</h2>

You can't easily tell what a second-hand seat has been through. One that's been in a crash, stored badly or is past its expiry date might not protect your child. As a rule:

- Don't buy second-hand unless you know exactly where it came from and its full history.
- Never use a seat that's been in a crash.
- Check for cracks, frayed harness straps and missing parts.
- Check the expiry date, and make sure it hasn't been recalled.

Look up any seat on [Product Safety Australia](https://www.productsafety.gov.au) for recalls before you buy.

<h2 id="faq">Car seat FAQ</h2>

### Do I need a capsule, or can I use a convertible seat from birth?
Many convertible seats are suitable from birth. A capsule is lighter and clicks onto a pram, which some parents find handy. Check the seat's size limits for newborns.

### When can I turn my child forward-facing?
The Australian Road Rules allow forward-facing from 6 months, but safety experts recommend keeping kids rearward-facing for as long as the seat allows. Check the seat's limits.

### When does my child need a booster seat?
Once they've outgrown the forward-facing harness limits. Keep them in a booster until the adult seatbelt fits properly.

### Can I use a car seat I bought overseas?
No. Seats used here must meet AS/NZS 1754, and an overseas seat that doesn't can't be used legally.

### Is a more expensive car seat safer?
Not necessarily. A higher price can mean extra features, but every seat sold here must meet the standard. Compare independent ratings and ease of use, and make sure it fits your car.

### How long does a car seat last?
Most have an expiry date set by the manufacturer. It's on the label, and you shouldn't use a seat that's past it.
